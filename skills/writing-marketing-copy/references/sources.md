# Sources: writing-marketing-copy

Why [writing-marketing-copy](../SKILL.md) holds what it holds: the research trail for maintainers. The skill states each rule as the catalog's own opinion; this file records which sources informed which rule, verbatim, so a rule can be re-checked. Web quotes were fetched and matched on 2026-11-05. Journal abstracts came from Crossref and OpenAlex. Book quotes were matched by Internet Archive full-text search in the named scan. Rule numbers refer to the skill.

## Why the rules spend words on information (the opening paragraph)

- Nielsen, [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/) (1997): "79 percent of our test users always scanned any new page they came across; only 16 percent read word-by-word." Small think-aloud studies: directional.
- Nielsen, [How Little Do Users Read?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) (2008): "users have time to read at most 28% of the words during an average visit; 20% is more likely." Log data, not eyetracking; an upper bound on reading time.
- Morkes & Nielsen, [Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/) (1997): "A sample Web site scored 58% higher in measured usability when it was written concisely, 47% higher when the text was scannable, and 27% higher when it was written in an objective style". Hedge kept: for the objective version only satisfaction was significant, and the "marketese" control already carried the visitor numbers, so the effect is of hype, not of missing data. n=51 across 5 conditions.

## Rule 1, the point first

- Pernice, [F-Shaped Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/) (2017): "Start headings and subheadings with the words carrying most information: if users see only the first 2 words, they should still get the gist of the following section."
- Nielsen, [First 2 Words](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/) (2009): "Users typically see about 2 words for most list items"; the worst-performing lead word was "Introducing".
- GOV.UK, [Clear structure](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-structure/): "Put the most important information first."
- Microsoft, [Top 10 tips](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/top-10-tips-style-voice): "Lead with what's most important. Front-load keywords for scanning."
- Counter-evidence weighed: people do read when the content pays ([Nielsen 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/website-reading/)). That weakens "nobody reads", not "put the point first".

## Rule 2, specific over vague

- Ganz & Grimes, *Journal of Advertising Research* 58(4), 2018, DOI [10.2501/JAR-2018-001](https://doi.org/10.2501/JAR-2018-001): "being specific increased the perceived credibility of green claims". Domain is green claims; generalising is an inference.
- Xie & Kronrod, *Journal of Advertising* 41(4), 2012, DOI [10.1080/00913367.2012.10672460](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2012.10672460): "Highly skeptical consumers are less likely to be affected by numerical precision." Hence the skill's "real and checkable".
- Nielsen, [Show Numbers as Numerals](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/) (2007): "numerals often stop the wandering eye and attract fixations".
- Strunk, [*The Elements of Style*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm) (1918), rule 12: "Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract."
- Not used: the claim that concrete wording makes statements seem truer (Hansen & Wänke 2010) failed a preregistered replication (Henderson et al., *Collabra* 2019, DOI [10.1525/collabra.192](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.192)).

## Rule 3, proof over claims

- Nielsen (1997, above): "Users detested 'marketese'… credibility suffers when users clearly see that the site exaggerates."
- Fogg, [Stanford Web Credibility Guidelines](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html) (2002): "Make it easy to verify the accuracy of the information on your site."
- Harley, [Trustworthy Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/) (2016): quotes and case studies were regarded "with a healthy dose of skepticism".
- Mailchimp, [Writing principles](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/writing-principles/): "Avoid dramatic storytelling and grandiose claims."
- The line the skill quotes: David Ogilvy, *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (Atheneum, 1963), "The more informative your advertising, the more persuasive it will be." Matched in Internet Archive scan [`confessionsofadv0000davi`](https://archive.org/details/confessionsofadv0000davi).

## Rules 4 and 5, headlines

- Loranger & Nielsen, [Microcontent](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/microcontent-how-to-write-headlines-page-titles-and-subject-lines/) (2017): "an ultrashort abstract of its associated content, written in plain language, with no puns, and no 'cute' or 'clever' wordings"; "Make sure the headline works out of context"; on taglines, "Say who you are and what you do quickly".
- Moran, [Tone of voice and users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/) (2016): "Funny or clever headlines often fail to serve the actual purpose of headlines".
- GOV.UK, [Clear titles](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-titles/): "Your title should be 65 characters or less (including spaces)." The limit rests on search truncation, not comprehension research, which is why the skill applies it to `<title>` only.
- Length disagreed, so the skill rules on density, not a word count: Ogilvy reported long news headlines outselling short ones in *Confessions* (1963) and lower readership for headlines over ten words in *Ogilvy on Advertising* (Vintage, 1985; scan [`ogilvyonadvertis0000ogil`](https://archive.org/details/ogilvyonadvertis0000ogil)).
- Teasers disagreed: clickbait features raised clicks in Kuiken et al., *Digital Journalism* 5(10), 2017, DOI [10.1080/21670811.2017.1279978](https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2017.1279978); NN/g argues they erode trust on repeat visits. Ruling: curiosity only when the headline still says what is below and the page delivers.

## Rule 6, active voice and owning the work

- GOV.UK, [Clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/): "Use the active voice rather than the passive voice", except "when the outcome is more important than the agent of the action".
- Nielsen, [Passive Voice Is Redeemed](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/passive-voice-is-redeemed-for-web/) (2007): passive "can let you front-load important keywords in headings, blurbs, and lead sentences."
- GOV.UK, [Right tone](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/right-tone/): "Do not assume the audience will know who the 'we' is."
- Krause, [UX Design Portfolios](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/) (2019, survey of 204 hiring managers): "Your specific role in the project and how you collaborated with others."
- No research compares "I" and "we" in portfolios, and Apple's first-person ban scopes itself to documentation and interfaces, so the pronoun is each brand's call and lives in its voice guide ([defining-a-voice](../../defining-a-voice/SKILL.md)). This file's sources back only "own the work" and "name the actor".

## Rule 7, filler and machine-made patterns

- GOV.UK, [A to Z, words to avoid](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/): "leverage (unless in the financial sense)"; "With all of these words you can generally replace them by breaking the term into what you're actually doing. Be open and specific."
- Microsoft, [leverage](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/l/leverage): "Don't use as a verb to mean take advantage of."
- Loranger, [Cringeworthy words](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/cringeworthy-words/) (2016): "Intensifiers rarely add value to a sentence"; stock openers "often sound inauthentic".
- Oppenheimer, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20(2), 2006, DOI [10.1002/acp.1178](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178): "a negative relationship between complexity and judged intelligence".
- Orwell, [Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) (1946): "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print."
- Machine-made patterns: [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing): "LLMs overuse the rule of three"; "'Not only … but …' or 'It is not just …, it's …'". Frequency studies: Kobak et al., *Science Advances* 2025, DOI [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813) ("delves", "showcasing"); Reinhart et al., *PNAS* 2025, DOI [10.1073/pnas.2422455122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422455122) ("tapestry"). These measure frequency, not reader harm, and people detect machine text no better than chance, hence "a self-edit list, never proof of who or what wrote a page".
- The "not just X, but Y" flag applies only where X is a strawman: the catalogue above names the construction as a pattern, not as an error in itself, and a contrast between two real facts carries information.
- "Seamless", "cutting-edge" and similar are banned by no first-party guide (absence control-tested against Microsoft's A–Z, where `leverage` resolves and they 404). Listing them is the catalog's call, under GOV.UK's "Be open and specific".

## Rule 8, one name per thing

- digital.gov, [Plain language: short and simple](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles/short-simple): "You don't need to use synonyms to make your writing more interesting."
- Microsoft, [Use simple words](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/word-choice/use-simple-words-concise-sentences): "Use one term consistently to represent one concept."
- Loranger, [Plain language for experts](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/) (2017): branded terms "should be replaced (or at least accompanied) by explicit terms."

## Case studies

- Krause (2019, above): the problem, "Your specific role", "Challenges you faced, including design concepts that were ultimately not pursued", "How the project affected the users and the business", "What you learned"; "Choose 3–5 Projects as Detailed Case Studies".
- Dykes, [UX copy sizes](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-copy-sizes/) (2025): long-form copy suits "Reports or case studies" and "About us pages".
- Strunk (1918), rule 13, on concision as density: "that he make every word tell."
